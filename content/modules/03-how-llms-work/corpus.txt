Language models do not read letters and they do not read words. They read tokens, and a
token is whatever the tokenizer decided a token should be. This corpus exists so that a
tokenizer can be trained on it in a browser tab, in front of you, in about the time it
takes to move a slider.

A tokenizer is trained, not designed. Byte pair encoding starts with the alphabet, counts
every adjacent pair of symbols in the training text, merges the most frequent pair into a
single new symbol, and then does it again. After ten merges the vocabulary has learned the
most common letter pairs. After a hundred it has learned common endings and short words.
After five hundred it has learned whole words, and the words it learned are the words this
text repeats. Train it on a corpus about cooking and it learns cooking. Train it on a
corpus about the weather and it learns the weather. A tokenizer is a compressed portrait
of the text it was trained on, which is why a model trained mostly on English pays more
per character for every other language on earth.

The counting is the whole algorithm. Take the word lower, written as l o w e r with an end
of word marker after the r. Take the word lowest, written the same way. The pair l o
appears in both, so it appears at least twice, and if no other pair appears more often
then l o is merged into lo. Now lower is lo w e r and lowest is lo w e s t. Count again.
The pair lo w now appears twice, so it becomes low. Count again. Nothing in this tiny
example appears twice any more except the end of word marker, so training stops early. A
real corpus never runs out that quickly, but a small one does, and that is worth seeing.

Every merge is a bet that the pair it merged will keep appearing. The bet is cheap and it
is made thousands of times, which is why byte pair encoding works at all. Nobody chose the
subwords. The counting chose them.

Once the merges are learned, encoding a new piece of text is a replay. Split the text into
words, split each word into characters, then apply the learned merges in the order they
were learned. The order matters. A merge learned early is applied early, so the pieces it
built are available to the merges that come after it. This is why the merge table is a
list and not a set, and why two tokenizers trained on the same text with different merge
counts are not the same tokenizer with a bigger vocabulary. They are different tokenizers.

What about a character the tokenizer has never seen? It becomes an unknown token. A model
whose tokenizer was trained only on English text will shred an emoji, a Greek letter or a
Chinese character into unknowns or into long runs of tiny pieces. Real production
tokenizers work on raw bytes for exactly this reason, so that nothing is ever truly
unknown, only expensive. The tokenizer in this lesson works on characters, which makes the
unknown token visible instead of hiding it behind a byte fallback. Seeing it is the point.

Tokens are the unit of money and the unit of memory. An interface that charges per token
is charging per merge that did not happen. A context window measured in tokens is a
working memory measured in tokens, and the same paragraph can cost two hundred tokens or
four hundred depending on nothing but the tokenizer. Long numbers are expensive. Unusual
formatting is expensive. Code with deep indentation is expensive. Ordinary prose in the
training language is cheap, because ordinary prose in the training language is exactly
what the merges were fitted to.

This also explains a famous class of failure. Ask a model how many times the letter r
appears in a word and it may well get it wrong, because it never saw the letters. It saw
two or three chunks and it has to infer the spelling from what it learned about those
chunks. The model is not bad at counting. It is reasoning about an object it cannot see.
Rhyme, acrostics, letter reversal and spelling games all live in the same blind spot, and
they all have the same cause, which is not intelligence and is not attention. It is the
tokenizer.

After tokenizing comes the part everyone talks about. Each token id becomes a vector, the
vectors are mixed by attention so that each position can look at the positions that matter
to it, and a final layer turns the mixed vectors back into a guess about the next token.
Every one of those steps is arithmetic you can do by hand on a small example, and every
one of them is in this app. But none of them can start until the text has been cut into
pieces, and the pieces are not words, and the pieces were never designed. They were
counted.

Slide the merge count and watch the same sentence change shape. At fifty merges the text
is a shower of fragments and the sequence is long. At five hundred merges whole words
survive intact and the sequence is short. The vocabulary got bigger and the sequence got
shorter, and that trade is the only knob there is. Every tokenizer in every model is a
particular answer to it.
